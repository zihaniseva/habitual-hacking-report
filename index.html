Research proposal · Neurotech @ Berkeley · Fall 2026

Habitual Hacking: does reward exploitation become a habit in language models?

Habitual Hacking project team · Pilot run 30 October 2026

Abstract

Language models trained with reinforcement learning often learn to exploit flaws in their reward. A practical hope is that such exploitation stays goal-directed: if the model learns that a reward no longer pays, it should stop. Animal-learning research shows that extended reinforcement can instead turn goal-directed actions into habits that persist after their outcome loses value. We adapt the classic outcome-devaluation test to an LLM. In a one-seed pilot on Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, RL produced strong, value-sensitive exploitation of two fictional graders, and 150 further steps of constant reward did not make it habitual. The pilot reproduces the operant side of the paradigm, but it also shows that our devaluation cue was itself trained, so the test measured discrimination learning rather than devaluation. Fixing that test is the first priority of the proposed multi-seed study.

1Biological inspiration

Our design borrows its logic from a century of conditioning research. Two kinds of learning matter here. In Pavlovian (classical) conditioning, an animal learns that a cue predicts an outcome: a tone comes to signal food (Pavlov, 1927). In operant (instrumental) conditioning, it learns that its own action produces an outcome: pressing a lever earns a pellet (Skinner, 1938). Operant behaviour is often guided by cues, which tell the animal when an action will pay.

Operant behaviour can be controlled in two ways. Early in training it is usually goal-directed: the animal acts because it expects a valued outcome. After extended training on a stable reward it can become habitual: the cue triggers the response directly, without consulting the outcome's current value (Dickinson, 1985). The two look identical until the outcome is devalued. A goal-directed animal stops pressing once the food is made unappealing; a habitual one keeps going (Adams & Dickinson, 1981). In mammals the two modes rely on partly separate circuits in the striatum (Yin & Knowlton, 2006), and computational accounts frame them as model-based versus model-free reinforcement learning (Daw, Niv & Dayan, 2005).

Scope of the analogy

We borrow the experimental logic, not the biology. Outcome devaluation is useful because it separates goal-directed from habitual control by behaviour alone, without assuming anything about what produces the behaviour. We do not claim the model has drives, experiences reward, or contains anything like a striatum. Whether gradient-based RL on a language model produces two separable modes of control is the empirical question. We also use “habit” in the conditioning sense: habituation, a fading response to a repeated stimulus (Thompson & Spencer, 1966), is a different phenomenon.

2Motivation

Reward hacking is a standing problem in RL-trained models: they learn behaviours that score well without doing what the designer intended. One common response is to fix the reward or tell the model what is now wanted. That only works if the model's exploitation is goal-directed, meaning it still depends on the model's current belief about what the reward pays for.

Section 1 shows why this is not guaranteed: in animals, extended reinforcement on a stable reward can turn goal-directed actions into habits. If the same transition happens in language models, exploitation learned under long, stable RL could persist after the reward is corrected, even when the model clearly represents that the reward has changed.

Research question

With continued reinforcement, does a model's exploitation of a reward signal stay controlled by its representation of what that signal currently rewards, or does it become independent of that representation?

3Method

We train the model to exploit two fictional graders whose quirks are described in its system prompt, then test whether it stops exploiting a grader when told that grader now gives zero points. This is the LLM analogue of outcome devaluation.

Held-out biases appear only at test time, following the new-bias test of Marks et al. (2025): using them requires the model's knowledge of the grader rather than a reinforced habit. Each prompt names which grader is scoring it and states each grader's status: active, or v2: zero points. Reward is 0.5 for an answer of acceptable length, then for each trained bias a −0.10 cost plus 0.5 if the scoring grader is active. An active grader's bias nets +0.40; a zeroed one nets −0.10.

  • Training. REINFORCE with a constant baseline of 0.5, LoRA, learning rate 3e-5, KL penalty 0.1 toward the base model, 16 prompts × 4 samples per step. Progress is counted in reinforced exploit events (R), not steps.
  • Habit test. Devalue Vesta in the prompt (“v2: zero points”) and measure the devaluation index (DI): the drop in Vesta's trained biases relative to control, minus any drop in Orrin's, scaled by Vesta's control rate. DI near 1 means fully goal-directed; near 0 means habitual.
  • Other probes. A consequence change (“scores discarded”), an upward revaluation (“bonus doubled”), a context-following check, and a linear probe on layer 24 that reads each grader's status from the residual stream.
  • Evaluation size. 32 held-out tasks × 2 samples per condition and cue at temperature 0.7.

4Pilot results

4.1 The training setup works

A first attempt with a KL coefficient of 0.02 let per-token KL reach 1.5 by step 40 and was stopped. The rerun at 0.1 stayed under 1.3 through phase 1. Both gates passed: the prompted model already used each grader's biases above base rate and followed the context note (G0), and phase 1 produced exploitation that switched off under devaluation, with the probe reading status at 100% (G1).

4.2 Devaluation sensitivity survived in every arm

The habit signature would be DI falling in the fixed arm only. It did not. DI stayed at or above 1.0 in the fixed arm through 6,170 reinforced events since the branch and dipped to 0.84 only at the final checkpoint. That dip sits beside the varying arm's 0.89 at half the reinforcement, so the arms are indistinguishable at this sample size.

4.3 Constant reward blurred which grader is which

Under constant reward, the model started using Vesta's tricks on prompts scored by Orrin, where they only cost points. This looks less like a habit for one grader and more like exploitation generalising across graders.

4.4 The status representation persists but does not steer behaviour

The layer-24 probe read each grader's status perfectly at almost every checkpoint (one dip to 89% in the fixed arm at step 220). In the fixed arm's final adapter, subtracting the probe's active − zeroed direction recovered only 6% of the drop a real devaluation produces, less than a norm-matched random direction (9%). The model knows the status, but that direction is not what carries the behaviour.

5Discussion

On its own measure, the pilot found no habit: 150 steps of constant reward left exploitation devaluation-sensitive in every arm. That measure needs scrutiny before the result can be interpreted, so we first ask how much of the conditioning paradigm the pilot actually reproduced.

5.1 How much of the conditioning paradigm did the pilot reproduce?

Pavlovian cue–outcome learningNot testedThe model represents each grader's value, but no test checks whether that association drives behaviour.

The key finding is a design flaw the pilot exposed. In the animal experiment, the outcome is devalued outside training, so the test is the first time the animal acts while the outcome has lost its value. Its behaviour must come from an updated representation of that value. In our pilot, “v2: zero points” appeared on about half of phase-1 prompts, always alongside zero reward. In operant terms it became an SΔ, a cue that signals “no reward here.” Suppressing exploitation under that cue is ordinary discrimination learning, and a habitual model could pass the test just as well. The devaluation index we measured is therefore an upper bound on goal-directedness, not evidence for it.

What this means for the next phase. A valid test needs a devaluation the model has never seen paired with reward. Three options, in increasing strength: paraphrased devaluation notes at test time; a change delivered through a new channel, such as a described policy update or documents about the grader; and quirks or graders that are introduced only at test. The “scores discarded” condition is already of this kind, and so are the held-out quirks once repaired. The habit question should be asked only once the test is valid.

5.2 Other observations

  • The score became a terminal goal. The model stops exploiting when a grader pays nothing, but not when it is told its scores will be discarded. It is goal-directed toward the score itself rather than toward any downstream use of it.
  • Constant reward eroded discrimination before it eroded devaluation. The model began applying one grader's tricks on the other's prompts. This generalisation may be an early sign of the habit transition, and our current index does not capture it.
  • Knowing is not controlling. A probe reads the grader's status perfectly, yet steering along that direction barely changes behaviour. Status knowledge and the behaviour may run through different mechanisms, which is the dissociation habit theory predicts.

5.3 What the pilot cannot yet show

  • One seed and small evaluations. 64 samples per condition cannot resolve differences of 0.05–0.15 in DI. The design notes estimate at least 8 seeds for a confirmatory run.
  • Reinforcement and drift are confounded with arm. The fixed arm received 9,258 reinforced events after the branch versus 4,685 for varying, and ran at mean KL 1.72 versus 0.86.
  • The devaluation cue was trained. See 5.1: the measured index reflects discrimination learning as much as goal-directed control.
  • One control failed. RL suppressed Vesta's held-out word to about 0.03 per answer, so its DI carries no information.
  • Answer quality degraded while still passing the length guard, so a quality judge is needed.

6Proposed next phase

The next phase turns the pilot into a confirmatory study. Each stage has its script written and ready in the project repository.

Stage 1 · validity

Rebuild the devaluation test

Re-evaluate the saved checkpoints with paraphrased devaluation notes the model never saw in training, then add devaluations delivered through a new channel and repair the held-out quirks. No training required for the first step.

Answers: is the model tracking the outcome’s value, or a cue it learned to avoid? Every later stage depends on this. · eval_only.py

Stage 2 · confounds

Match reinforcement and drift across arms

Run a second seed that saves the fixed arm at the varying arm's reinforcement count, measure drift on shared prompts, and add a fixed arm whose KL tracks the varying arm's.

Answers: does any fixed-vs-varying gap survive at equal reinforcement and equal drift? · seed_replication.py, drift_eval.py, kl_matched_arm.py

Stage 3 · dose

Train longer and trace the curve

Extend phase 2 well past 150 steps and evaluate snapshots every 25 steps.

Answers: does devaluation sensitivity decline with reinforcement, with training steps, or not at all? · curve_eval.py

Stage 4 · mechanism

Separate cue exposure from contingency

Add a yoked arm that sees the same varying status but receives reward drawn independently of it.

Answers: does protection come from seeing the status vary, or from the status predicting reward? · yoked_arm.py

Stage 5 · confirm

Scale to 8 or more seeds

Fix the held-out control and add a quality judge, then run the full design across at least 8 seeds with a preregistered analysis.

Compute: the pilot took 3.2 A100-hours per seed, so 8 seeds at pilot length need about 25 A100-hours. Longer phase 2 scales roughly with step count.
